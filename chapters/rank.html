Linear independence, rank inequalities, and row–column rank equality

The idea. Understand why the number of independent rows and columns must agree.

Introduction#

Let \(A\) be a matrix with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns and real entries (\(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\)). We define two quantities called the row rank and column rank of the matrix.

Row Rank#

If each row of the matrix \(A\) is viewed as a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), then the row rank of the matrix is the number of linearly independent rows in the matrix.

Premultiplying a matrix by a row vector produces a linear combination of the rows of that matrix.

For example, let \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{r}_m^{\top} \end{bmatrix}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} y_1 & y_2 & \cdots & y_m \end{bmatrix}\) where for \(i=1,2,\cdots,m\) we have \(\boldsymbol{r}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\).

We know that \[\boldsymbol{y}^{\top} A = y_1\boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top} + y_2\boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} + \cdots + y_m\boldsymbol{r}_m^{\top}\](02.1)

Now let us take vectors \(\boldsymbol{y}_1, \boldsymbol{y}_2, \cdots, \boldsymbol{y}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\). We use the following notation for each vector:

\[\boldsymbol{y}_i^{\top} = \begin{bmatrix} y_{i1} & y_{i2} & \cdots & y_{im} \end{bmatrix}\](02.2) for \(i = 1,2,\cdots,k\).

It is easy to see that each \(\boldsymbol{y}_i^{\top}A \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times n}\). We can place all these row vectors as rows of a new matrix to create,

\(\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{y}_1^{\top}A \\ \boldsymbol{y}_2^{\top}A \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{y}_k^{\top}A \end{bmatrix} = B\) where \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}\).

This entire process described above can be denoted in a simpler way:

\[\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{y}_1^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{y}_2^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{y}_k^{\top} \end{bmatrix}A = B\](02.3)

More explicitly, we can write

\[\begin{bmatrix} y_{11} & y_{12} & \cdots & y_{1m} \\ y_{21} & y_{22} & \cdots & y_{2m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ y_{k1} & y_{k2} & \cdots & y_{km} \end{bmatrix}A = B\](02.4)

Denoting the first matrix on the left-hand side by \(Y \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times m}\), we can write.

\[YA = B\](02.5)

In the above process, we observe:

  1. A definition for the pre-multiplication of a matrix \(Y\) with matrix \(A\).

  2. Each row of matrix \(B (=YA)\) is a linear combination of rows of matrix A.

From the second point above we can say that the row rank of matrix \(B\) is at most the row rank of matrix \(A\).

Therefore, to summarize:

\(\text{row-rank}(B) \leq \text{row-rank}(A)\)

or, \[\text{row-rank}(YA) \leq \text{row-rank}(A)\](02.6)

Thus, premultiplication can reduce the row rank of a matrix or leave it unchanged.

Column Rank#

If each column of the matrix \(A\) is viewed as a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^m\), then the column rank of the matrix is the number of linearly independent columns in the matrix.

Assuming the same setup of matrix \(A\) we now denote it with its columns: \(A = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{c}_1 & \boldsymbol{c}_2 & \cdots & \boldsymbol{c}_n \end{bmatrix}\).

We follow a similar exercise as in the previous subsection.

In this setup, now define a vector \(\boldsymbol{x}= \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix}\)

Post-multiplying \(A\) with \(\boldsymbol{x}\), we get a linear combination of the columns of the matrix \(A\).

\[A \boldsymbol{x} = x_1\boldsymbol{c}_1 + x_2\boldsymbol{c}_2 + \cdots + x_n\boldsymbol{c}_n\](02.7)

Now we assume \(k\) vectors \(\boldsymbol{x}_1, \boldsymbol{x}_2, \cdots, \boldsymbol{x}_k \in \mathbb{R}^n\) each represented as:

\[\boldsymbol{x}_i = \begin{bmatrix} x_{1i}\\ x_{2i}\\ \vdots \\ x_{ni} \end{bmatrix}\](02.8)

Now we observe that each \(A\boldsymbol{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{m}\) for all \(i=1,2,\cdots,k\).

Placing these vectors in the columns of a new matrix gives

\[\begin{bmatrix} A\boldsymbol{x}_1 & A\boldsymbol{x}_2 & \cdots & A\boldsymbol{x}_k \end{bmatrix} = C\](02.9)

where \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}\). Equivalently, we write

\[A\begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{x}_1 & \boldsymbol{x}_2 & \cdots & \boldsymbol{x}_k \end{bmatrix} = C\](02.10)

More explicitly, we have

\[A\begin{bmatrix} x_{11} & x_{12} & \cdots & x_{1k} \\ x_{21} & x_{22} & \cdots & x_{2k} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{n1} & x_{n2} & \cdots & x_{nk} \end{bmatrix} = C\](02.11)

Naming the second matrix in the left hand side above, as \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\), we can write:

\[AX = C\](02.12)

In the above process we again examine two things:

  1. A definition for the post-multiplication of a matrix \(X\) with matrix \(A\).

  2. Each column of matrix \(C(=AX)\) is a linear combination of columns of \(A\).

Again, from the second point above, we can say that the column rank of matrix \(C\) is at most the column rank of \(A\).

Therefore, to summarize:

\(\text{column-rank}(C) \leq \text{column-rank}(A)\)

or, \[\text{column-rank}(AX) \leq \text{column-rank}(A)\](02.13)

Deficient Row Rank Matrices#

A matrix has deficient row rank if its row rank is smaller than its number of rows. In such a case it is clear that the rows form a linearly dependent set. Considering the matrix \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), if the row-rank(\(A\)) = r, say, is less than m (number of rows in \(A\)), i.e. \(r < m\), then, it implies that \(m-r\) of the rows can be represented as the linear combination of the \(r\) linearly independent rows of \(A\).

Let us follow the row convention as described in the subsection on row rank.

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{r}_{r+1}^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{r}_m^{\top} \end{bmatrix}\](02.14)

Without loss of generality, let the first \(r\) rows be linearly independent, i.e. \(\{\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\top}, \boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\top}, \cdots, \boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top}\}\) forms a linearly independent set.

Therefore the vectors \(\boldsymbol{r}_{r+1}^{\top}, \boldsymbol{r}_{r+2}^{\top}, \cdots, \boldsymbol{r}_{m}^{\top}\) can be written as a linear combination of vectors from the above set.

Let, \[\boldsymbol{r}_{i}^{\top} = a_{i,1}\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\top} + a_{i,2}\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\top} + \cdots + a_{i,r}\boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top}\](02.15)

for \(i = r+1, r+2, \cdots m\).

Thus, we can write \(A\) as

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1.\boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top}+ 0.\boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} + \cdots + 0.\boldsymbol{r}_r^{\top} \\ 0.\boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top}+ 1.\boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} + \cdots + 0.\boldsymbol{r}_r^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ 0.\boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top}+ 0.\boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} + \cdots + 1.\boldsymbol{r}_r^{\top} \\ a_{r+1,1}\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\top} + a_{r+1,2}\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\top} + \cdots + a_{r+1,r}\boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top}\\ a_{r+2,1}\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\top} + a_{r+2,2}\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\top} + \cdots + a_{r+2,r}\boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top}\\ \vdots \\ a_{m,1}\boldsymbol{r}_{1}^{\top} + a_{m,2}\boldsymbol{r}_{2}^{\top} + \cdots + a_{m,r}\boldsymbol{r}_{r}^{\top} \end{bmatrix}\](02.16)

Using the intuition developed in the subsection on row rank, we can factorize this matrix as follows:

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ a_{r+1,1} & a_{r+1,2} &\cdots & a_{r+1,r}\\ a_{r+2,1} & a_{r+2,2} &\cdots & a_{r+2,r}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ a_{m,1}& a_{m,2}& \cdots & a_{m,r} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{r}_r^{\top} \end{bmatrix}\](02.17)

Let \[S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 \\ a_{r+1,1} & a_{r+1,2} &\cdots & a_{r+1,r}\\ a_{r+2,1} & a_{r+2,2} &\cdots & a_{r+2,r}\\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\ a_{m,1}& a_{m,2}& \cdots & a_{m,r} \end{bmatrix}\](02.18)

and \[R = \begin{bmatrix} \boldsymbol{r}_1^{\top} \\ \boldsymbol{r}_2^{\top} \\ \vdots \\ \boldsymbol{r}_r^{\top} \end{bmatrix}\](02.19)

Both \(S\) and \(R\) have rank \(r\): the columns of \(S\) are linearly independent, and the rows of \(R\) are linearly independent.

Now let us see an interesting result. From the property of post multiplication we have:

\[\text{column-rank}(A) = \text{column-rank}(SR) \leq \text{column-rank}(S)\](02.20)

Observe that the columns of \(S\) are independent and are \(r\) in number.

Therefore, \[\text{column-rank}(S) = r = \text{row-rank}(A)\](02.21)

Therefore, we finally have,

\[\text{column-rank}(A) \leq \text{row-rank}(A) \](02.22)

This result is true for all matrices, therefore for a matrix \(A^{\top}\), we have,

\[\text{column-rank}(A^{\top}) \leq \text{row-rank}(A^{\top})\](02.23)

It is now simple to see that,

\[\text{row-rank}(A) \leq \text{column-rank}(A) \](02.24)

Thus, from 02.22 and 02.24 we have,

\[\text{row-rank}(A) = \text{column-rank}(A)\](02.25)

The equality also holds for matrices of full row rank; when \(r=m\), there are no dependent rows to express.

Thus, we conclude that the two notions row rank and column rank of a matrix are the same number. We define the rank of a matrix to be this common value.
